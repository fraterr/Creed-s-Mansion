Turn Queue
& Tactics

A romhack for Shining Force (USA) · v13

Shining Force rolls the turn order at the start of every round, from each unit's agility, and then keeps it entirely to itself. You act, something happens, and you find out who moves next when they move. Thirty years of players have compensated by guessing.

This patch stops the guessing. It puts a strip along the bottom of the battlefield showing the next six combatants in the exact order the engine will call them — drawn with their own sprites, not icons — and it keeps going into the round after that. Once that was working, it turned out the game was hiding a few other things too, so those got fixed as well.

Four features, roughly three kilobytes of patch, and not one original byte of the ROM moved out of place.

What it adds

1 · The turn queue

Six slots, left to right, in the order the engine will actually call them. Each combatant is drawn with their real battlefield sprite, so you read the bar the same way you read the map: that's Tao, that's a Goblin, that's your healer standing three places too late.

The unit whose turn it is blinks. Everyone in the bar keeps their idle walk animation, so it never looks like a row of stickers. When the current round runs out of combatants the bar keeps filling with the next round, drawn mirrored so you can tell at a glance where this round ends and the following one begins — and the prediction is the engine's own, not a guess.

It also knows when to get out of the way. If the command menu, a dialogue window or the cursor would collide with it, the bar hides itself and comes back — with a short debounce, so flipping between a menu and its submenu doesn't make it flicker. When the battle ends and the game returns to walking around the map, the bar disappears entirely.

2 · Choose your difficulty

Start a new game, name your hero, and Simone asks one more question before she sends you off. The three answers arrive in the same bouncing speech balloons the game already uses to pick a save slot — because they are those balloons, widened by two cells and repointed at new text.

  • NORMAL — the game exactly as Camelot shipped it.
  • HARD — every enemy gets +25% attack, defense and HP.
  • OUCH! — +50%. Runefaust stops being a formality.

The scaling is applied to the enemy table each time a battle map is initialised, so it holds for fresh battles and for battles you resume after a defeat. Your choice lives in a spare byte inside the save block, which means each adventure remembers its own setting, and a save made before the patch simply reads as Normal.

3 · Kills and deaths, per character

The status screen has always had a conspicuous gap between the portrait and the GOLD box. It now holds a running tally: how many enemies this character has personally put down, and how many times they've been carried to the church.

Counting turned out to be the interesting part. The engine runs its death routine four times per casualty — once for each pass of the dissolve animation — so a naive counter cheerfully reported four kills for one goblin. The patch only counts the pass where the dying unit still exists.

Known limitation: the counters live in work RAM and reset when you power off. Making them survive a save means finding twenty more spare bytes inside the save block, which is a job for a later version.

4 · Experience that doesn't evaporate

In the original game, a character sitting at 99 EXP who kills something worth 20 does not end up at 19. They end up at 0. The overflow is thrown away, and over a full campaign that's a genuinely painful amount of lost progress — entirely invisible, because the game never shows you the number it discarded.

The patch stashes the surplus at the moment the level-up clamp fires, then writes it back after the level has been granted. 99 + 20 now becomes level up, then 19 EXP. It is the smallest change in the whole hack — six bytes at one site and eight at another — and probably the one you'll feel most over a playthrough.

Under the hood

Everything here is built on SF1DISASM, a full annotated disassembly of the game that reassembles bit-for-bit into the original ROM. That bit-for-bit property is the whole game: if a build stops matching for reasons you didn't intend, you've broken something.

The one rule: never move a byte

Shining Force is full of pointers baked into data — sprite tables, script offsets, tileset addresses — that were computed at build time in 1992 and are not recalculated at runtime. Insert four bytes in the middle of a bank and everything after it shifts, every baked pointer becomes wrong, and the game greets you with a beautifully corrupted intro. (Ask me how I know.)

So every hook in this patch is a same-size instruction replacement: a jsr that occupies exactly as many bytes as the instructions it displaces, padded with nop where needed. The replaced instructions are re-executed at the top of the new routine. All the actual new code lives in the free space at the end of banks 02 and 08, which the original build left as padding. The result is a patch that touches fourteen small regions of a 1.5 MB ROM and moves nothing.

Where the new state lives

New variables sit in a verified-unused stretch of work RAM — the queue's double-buffered turn order, the visibility debounce, the tile shadow used to repair the background the bar draws over, the kill and death arrays. The difficulty byte is the exception: it deliberately sits in an unused byte inside the block the game writes to SRAM, which is what makes it persist per-save for free.

Borrowing the game's own engines

Almost nothing here is drawn from scratch. The queue window is assembled from the same window tiles the game uses everywhere (in mirror and flip combinations that genuine windows never produce, so the bar can recognise and clean up after itself). The balloons are the save-slot balloons. Simone's question is typed through the real dialogue engine: the patch walks the ROM's own ASCII-to-glyph table, calls the routine that draws one variable-width glyph at the current text position, and then the routine that does one typewriter step — which is why it bleeps, respects the message-speed setting, and fast-forwards when you hold a button, without any of that being reimplemented.

How it was tested

Blind-patching a ROM and hoping is not a workflow. Development ran against a headless libretro front-end driving Genesis Plus GX: scripted controller input by frame number, PNG frame captures, work RAM and VRAM dumps, and direct reads of the 68000 register file to catch deadlocks. Every release is checked with a binary diff of the patched ROM against the original (the changed regions must be exactly the expected ones and nothing else), and with a frame-by-frame comparison of the intro against a vanilla baseline — twelve reference frames that must match pixel for pixel.

Two bugs found that way are worth mentioning, because neither would have been obvious from reading the code. The window-drawing routine clobbers d6 and d7 — the registers I was using as loop counters — which turned a three-iteration loop into a runaway. And the message engine leaves a continuation routine armed in the vertical-blank handler, which deadlocks anything that waits for vblank while drawing its own windows. Both produced a frozen screen and no other clue.

Applying the patch

  • Get an IPS patcher: Flips (Floating IPS) on Windows, macOS or Linux, or Lunar IPS if you prefer the classic. Most emulator front-ends can do it too.
  • Point it at ShiningForce_TurnQueue_v13.ips and at your unmodified Shining Force (USA).bin. Let it write a new file rather than patching in place, so you keep a clean original.
  • Load the result in any Mega Drive emulator, or on hardware via flash cart. The patched ROM is the same size as the original and the header checksum is corrected, so strict emulators and real hardware are both happy.
  • Start a New Game to see the difficulty prompt. Existing saves keep working and play at Normal.

Controls for the difficulty prompt: Left / Right to move between the balloons, C or A to confirm.
